Evaluating American Made: It's Complicated

Cliff and Jonah —
C: “Got a new shirt! Check it out. Uses the American Eagle to show it’s an American product. Cool design, isn’t it?”
J: “Let me guess. The tag reads ‘Made in Bangladesh.’”

Annie and Tyrel —
T: “I mean, let’s take a powerhouse American company. Adobe, for example.”
A: “Okay. Software, right?”
T: “Based in California, I believe. Huge company. Lots of dynamic growth.”
A: “So, an American company success story?”
T: “But here’s the thing: a third of their employees are from India. Almost half of all Finance and Operations staff positions are in India. Every year, employment in India is climbing, and so is Adobe’s revenue per employee. It’s like the Ship of Theseus applied to American commerce. Except the ship’s replacement parts are from a different ship entirely.”

Bart and Gabriella —
B: “So if a company wants to be considered truly American, it needs to meet the ‘all or virtually all’ standard.”
G: “People generally expect that, yes. But I think the middle ground should be acknowledged more.”
B: “What do you mean?”
G: “If you’re a business started by a US citizen on American soil, and you’re doing all you can to employ in the US and to source material in the US, shouldn’t that be celebrated too?”
B: “Sure.”
G: “Even if the owner had to get some of their material from overseas to reduce production costs, but they have strictly American employees and would source from America if it was possible, wouldn’t you want to support that American company also?”

Perhaps you’ve heard, or had, these sorts of discussions in recent years. Needless to say, the “American-Made” claims are murky right now. American buyers that want to promote American commerce the best way that they know how — with their dollar — often feel deceived, overwhelmed by vague legal jargon, or frustrated that there are American Makers left out of the picture.

This is a problem we aim to address head on at Homefront Markets. The source of the confusion (and subsequent frustration) is an oversimplified evaluation. There are really two core metrics behind the calculus on an American Made product: Who made it and What it’s made of. This is a distinction of labor from material. When we make the effort to separate them, we can more clearly assess the quality of domestic commerce claims, and it enables us to celebrate everything that is on the right track.

At Homefront Markets, we’ve founded our HFM Certification on this dual-metric insight. The following three certifications are designed to promote transparency and to make sure no one falls between the cracks.

That shirt of Cliff's wouldn't earn any recognition with us, no matter what's branded next to the logo, if the labor and materials are Bangladeshi.

Bronze, Silver, and Gold are all valuable, and each certification is intended to reflect value to you. Honest acknowledgement of a given Maker’s American-ness: this is what is missing from the resources available to the Patriot Buyer. Our evaluation strives for precision. Gold is killing it, Silver is working hard to get there, and Bronze is precisely the business Gabriella was describing. Integrity. Probity. Totality. We hope our certifications earn your trust.

So you might be asking: does Homefront Markets just take the company’s word for it? Yes — sort of. We are, of course, committed to having a vetted set of recommendations for all of you American Buyers out there, but we have to start somewhere. So we rely initially on basic web research to evaluate any given company against our HFM Certification. From there, we work to further verify these findings through direct contact with staff, feedback from buyers in the area, and a deep dive into their product line.

Rest assured, Homefront Markets will pull every lever to ensure quality and integrity in this process. Subscribers who have reason to believe that a company is misrepresented by the HFM Certification we’ve awarded them are strongly encouraged to contact us here with an explanation of what they know. Every flagged certification will trigger re-evaluation — the kind of accountability called for by Tyrel’s criticism of Adobe’s recent hiring practices.

If you’d like to read more about our evaluation process, subscribers’ commonly asked questions, and the specifics behind our questionnaire, keep reading here.

Be safe out there — and, as always, stay American.

Bronze

American hands. Every employee is a U.S. citizen, start to finish.

Ex: A furniture workshop in Ohio that is staffed entirely by local hires, but the hardwood comes from Canada

Silver

American materials. 75% or more of all product materials used are sourced from the United States and its territories, regardless of who's on the payroll.

Ex: A cookware brand smelting its own U.S.-mined aluminum, though its call center is overseas.

Gold

Of America, By America, For America. All U.S. workers; all U.S. materials. (Complies with the FTC’s “all or virtually all” standard by default).

Ex: A boot maker tanning domestic leather and sewing it in a Tennessee factory staffed by U.S. workers.
