On 250 years of American commerce: twenty-five moments that built the marketplace we inherited

Dear Reader,

We just exited July of 2026 – celebrating our nation’s 250th. For 25 decades, this country has proven time and time again its dedication to life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness (of virtue, that is). There is no better mantra than this, pulled directly from our Declaration of Independence, to point to how fundamental the American commercial interest is in understanding what it means to be a free American people.

Benjamin Franklin once sat to list thirteen virtues most worthy of pursuit. Virtues 5-7 are especially relevant to life in the marketplace: Frugality, Industry, and Sincerity. When we participate in the ebb and flow of this great nation’s economy, we practice those virtues as an expression of our lives, our liberties, and our moral pursuits. This year, of all years, is a most prudent one to choose to remember and celebrate the successes of America down through the decades.

It is in the spirit of this gratitude and remembrance that we want to take your through a journey across those decades and give brief mention to notable moments in the market on the homefront. Feel free to skim through and celebrate with us the wins that are most telling and inspiring to you.

25 wins – 1776-2026

The Benjamin Franklin National Memorial.

Founding Frontier: 1776-1825

Securing commercial independence through early maritime trade and agrarian expansion.

1781: The Bank of North America was chartered in Philadelphia, the first true commercial bank in the U.S. Thanks to the great Robert Morris, this bank, chartered by the Continental Congress, literally saved the country from wartime bankruptcy and restored faith in a new specie-backed currency.

1790: Employing *cough* innovative techniques to circumvent British bans on sharing plans for textile machinery, Samuel Slater memorized the complex workings of the water-powered cotton spinning mill by sight alone. Slater founded his own textile mill in Pawtucket, Rhode Island – the beginning of the American textile industry.

1803: President Jefferson secured the grandest land deal in history (no contest). The initial deal offered no more than $10 million for a strip of West Florida containing the all important Port of New Orleans. A desperate Napolean made a different offer: buy the whole Louisiana Territory for $15 Million. Jefferson believed in the vivacity of American commerce, and he took the deal, doubling the country and securing rich agricultural land, timbers, minerals, and control of the fourth largest watershed in the world.

1807: River navigation was a vital source of transportation for various persons and commodities in the frontier period. Robert Fulton crafted the Clermont in what was the first commercially successful steam-powered water vessel. The added efficiency made freighting costs plummet by 90%, and it spurred on a lumber and coal boom.

1825: William Henry Ashley organizes the first Rocky Mountain Rendezvous on Henry’s Fork, Green River in southwestern Wyoming. The event attracted Mountain Men and Native Americans from across The West to trade pelts, supplies, firearms, and whiskey.

Engine 119 at Golden Spike National Historical Park.

Industrial Awakening: 1826-1875

Linking a fractured nation with the power of steam, iron, and electricity.

1827: The Thomas brothers (Quaker businessmen) and George Brown met to discuss how Baltimore might keep up with commercial competition caused by canal access to the Ohio River Valley. They make a bold decision to build a double track railroad over land to the Ohio River – 350 miles away. The B&O was a true investment gamble, the first long-distance passenger or freight railroad in the world, and over mountains no less.

1844: Samuel Morse, an artist with a growing interest in technology and invention, took a gamble on a new career path. Partnering with a chemistry professor, a young tool maker, and a former Yale classmate, Morse ran a series of short experiments. Petitioning the House Committee on commerce for funding, Morse secured the money to test a 50-mile line. The Panic of 1837 and installation problems almost derailed him entirely, but he pushed on. He sent “What hath God wrought” from Washington to Baltimore.

1851: The sewing machine was revolutionized by Isaac Singer, who introduced the vertical presser foot, a horizontal table, and a continuous foot treadle. The innovations brought the sewing machine from the factory to the household, which would go on to have a major impact on the home economy for decades to come.

1859: George Bissell and Jonathan Eveleth see opportunity in refining crude oil for kerosene lamp light. They turned to Edwin Drake, an unemployed railway conductor and called him “Colonel Drake” to gain confidence from other investors. No one in the world knew how to drill for oil. Drake’s attempts met with failure after failure. Doubters dubbed his work “Drake’s Folly.” Then Drake tried a brilliant casing drill technique, and he hit it, changing the world forever.

1869: The Transcontinental Railroad project was a divisive one in the lead up to the Civil War. Lincoln saw the railroad as a vital wartime measure, but he couldn’t afford the material cost for it until after the war ended in 1865. Ultimately, this grand commercial project employed former Union and Confederate soldiers side by side with Irish and Chinese immigrants. The two rail lines were stitching a fractured nation back together, and driving the Golden Spike was the first mass media event in United States history.

The early light bulb.

Mass Market: 1876-1925

Harnessing assembly lines, mass migration, and Wall Street capital to achieve at scale.

1876: Alexander Graham Bell patented and tested his telephone - an invention stemming from his harmonic telegraph experiments. Bell’s invention would be rejected by the Western Union telegraph company as a mere “scientific toy”, but Bell would go on to found his own telephone company, which eventually evolved into AT&T.

1886: Coca-Cola was invented as a medicinal tonic and temperance drink in Atlanta. Georgia. Pemberton, a pharmacist, sold the 5-cent drink for headache and fatigue. The beverage would evolve and go on to become the most popular drink in the world.

1902: James Cash Penney invests his savings and a large loaned sum in a store that he and his two partners named “The Golden Rule Store.” His business was strictly cash only, devoted to a one-price policy over haggling, and strove for value pricing, despite an opportunity to gouge customers in mining and logging towns. J.C. Penney became a major retail success story.

1913: The moving assembly line implemented in Henry Ford’s Highland Park car plant was a tipping point in mass production. The Model T dropped from a 12 hour build time to 90 minutes. Only then was the car able to become an affordable household tool.

1920: KDKA (a Westinghouse callsign) began to schedule regular commercial broadcasting to create more demand for items already sold through irregular announcements on radio airwaves. The strategy would change marketing forever.

Vehicle innovation drives down price of cars - Shaniko, Oregon.

Pax Americana: 1926-1975

Navigating war and depression to anchor the Western world's postwar prosperity.

1930: King Kullen Grocery Co. was founded to provide a place for cash strapped families during the Great Depression to buy basic groceries. It is credited as the world’s first true supermarket, employing high-volume, low-margin pricing on a variety of goods.

1940: Packard Motor Car Company agreed to manufacture the highly complex British Rolls-Royce Merlin aircraft engine. Packard engineers spent months completely redrawing 2,000 British blueprints from scratch to adapt them from hand-fitted assembly to American high-speed mass production.

1955: Ray Kroc was a 52-year-old milkshake machine salesman struggling to sell his "Multimixers." He saw a small drive-in burger joint in San Bernardino, California running a highly efficient system with a stripped down menu for “speedee” service. Kroc saw the potential for a national franchise and pitched it to the McDonald brothers.

1956: The Federal-Aid Highway Act authorized 41,000 miles of controlled-access expressways, an investment of $26 Billion. The move was transformative for modern supply chains and shipping goods across the nation. The system is just over 1% of US road mileage overall, but it came to carry almost 25% of all US traffic. It is estimated that every $1 spent building it returned $6 in production for the economy.

1971: The establishment of Amtrak as an inner-city transport alternative put a spotlight on the failing railroad network. It became clear that the overbearing regulations of the ICC were keeping the transportation industry from adapting to the realities of modern markets. In its wake, several legislative moves were made to deregulate the sector and allow the market economy to rule the day.

Apple’s second attempt at a GUI-based personal computer.

Digital Curve: 1976-2026

Pursuing value, work, and communication in an era of rapid technological development.

1976: Steve Jobs convinces his friend and former classmate, Steve Wozniak, to market his newly designed micro circuit board as a fully assembled unit. They registered the Apple Computer Company, and they pooled $1,300 in working capital to fund it. Apple I was first produced in Jobs’ parents’ garage, and the bare circuit board retailed initially for $666.66. Apple became a tech giant, building computers for the consumer.

1991: Tim Berners-Lee posted a summation of the WorldWideWeb Project on the alt.hypertext Usenet newsgroup. The subsequent release of the browser and server code utilized for it laid the groundwork for an open, free, and collaborative community of users to build the web as we know it today.

1996: As the first major update to American Telecommunications law in more than 60 years, the Telecommunications Act of 1996 served as a catalyst to the transition from dial-up to high speed broadband connections. Because of these breakthroughs, America dominated the world in internet-based commerce.

2008: Early in the year, Brian Chesky and Joe Gebbia partnered with Nathan Bleckarczyk to launch Airbnb, the official launch capitalizing on attendees to the DNC in Denver, Colorado. After a series of creative funding efforts, the platform began to aggressively grow at scale. The platform was the first of a series to define the new “gig economy” – redefining traditional economic and investment systems altogether.

2026: The Universal Commerce Protocol was unveiled by Google’s Sundar Pichai alongside a team formed from several adjacent web-commerce companies. The move toward the new UCP standard enabled AI Agentic commercial work on the web using a machine-to-machine security check. The machines could then communicate directly through JSON-RPC data layers, paving the way for an explosion in Agentic Entrepreneurship.

This, of course, is no more than a sampling of the successes and wins our 250 years of history have brought about. The lives, liberties, and pursuits of astounding Americans on through the decades have culminated in a country that still offers the greatest opportunities and freedoms the world has ever had to offer. We should be deeply grateful for shoulders we now are invited to stand on.

"As Americans, commercial opportunities are all around us. The will to seize it is in our blood."

Follow in the footsteps of the great Makers of our time. Go out and build something incredible. And when you see someone else doing just that, applaud and support them.
